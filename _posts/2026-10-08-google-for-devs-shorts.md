**Score: 18/25.** [Google for Developers](https://www.youtube.com/@GoogleDevelopers/shorts) has published short-form video almost daily since April 2022. The videos that perform are the ones that teach nothing, sell nothing and mention no product. That isn't sloppiness. It's what the content is for — and once you see why, the thing worth criticising changes completely.

| Pillar | Score | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The Goal | 4/5 | A coherent goal with real destinations behind it, just not the one written on the channel |
| 2. Audience & Format Bet | 4/5 | Near-perfect fit for an audience Google never claims to serve |
| 3. The Craft | 3/5 | Genuine per-platform curation; one well-built playlist in a sea of unsorted shorts |
| 4. The Trust Signal | 3/5 | Credibility and reach pull in opposite directions, structurally |
| 5. The Engine | 4/5 | Three years of daily output across three platforms, with no visible measurement |

**What to steal:** build for the developer's identity, not their job queue. Curate per platform instead of cross-posting. And resist the call to action — the restraint is why it doesn't read as advertising.

**What they get wrong:** the content earning the reach can't build the association they need.

---

While researching my master's thesis in content strategy, I scrolled past a post from AWS Developers on LinkedIn: developers interviewed at re:Invent about their technology preferences. Spaces or tabs. Something deliberately trivial. It was obviously a corporate account and it didn't fully look like one.

That led me to [Google for Developers](https://www.youtube.com/@GoogleDevelopers/shorts), doing the same thing but slicker. Those two channels took up a large chunk of my thesis, and convinced me enough that I introduced the format at the company I was working at.

A year on, I wanted to look at it again with fresh eyes. Tech influencers making short-form do one of three things: comment on industry news, mock corporate tech, or teach something. Corporate developer content does none of them. So what is it doing?

## Three videos

Google's channel description states the deal: subscribe to "join a community of creative developers and learn the latest in Google technology." Community, and learning. Hold onto that.

Now three videos, same channel, same format.

A developer filmed like a documentary confession, because they [force-pushed to prod at 4:55 on a Friday](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/8uncdjpygSU): around 40,000 views. A useful explainer on [exposing your site's actions to AI agents via WebMCP](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/eiRlCVtzlm4): a fraction of that. Behind-the-scenes footage from a real Google developer event, ["POV: you're at I/O Connect Berlin"](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/5MFWcubNvtM): almost nobody.

So, if we look at the view count, the content promising community underperforms. The content delivering technology underperforms. And the content doing neither wins by two to three times.

![Three Google for Developers Shorts side by side, with the relatable joke, the WebMCP technical explainer, and the I/O Connect Berlin community clip.](/google-for-devs-three-shorts.png)

## Pillar 1 — The Goal: 4/5

I went looking for a hidden goal and found one sitting in plain sight — just not in the videos.

Follow where the shorts *should* lead and there are real destinations. [developers.google.com](https://developers.google.com/) opens with an invitation to "unlock AI models to build innovative apps," backed by a Learn section stacked with Gemini solutions. On LinkedIn, Google pushes developers into a Google Developer Community Discord — with a trackable link on it.

None of that is in the shorts. The only call to action is a subscribe prompt in the description, which nobody really reads (only me, 'cause it's my job). That's not an oversight. The shorts are the attention layer; the selling happens on the website and the community happens on Discord.

It also isn't recruiting, which was my first assumption. Google keeps that on [a separate channel](https://www.youtube.com/lifeatgoogle) that says outright it's hiring. And Google's own developer relations writing draws the line explicitly: [Reto Meier](https://medium.com/google-developers) argues DevRel "can't be cheerleaders, or sales people in disguise."

Coherent, resourced, honestly stated. Just not the goal written on the channel — and nothing connects one layer to the next.

Which raises the question that took me longest to answer: why publish every day for three years and ask for nothing but attention?

## Pillar 2 — Audience & Format Bet: 4/5

In my thesis, I built developer personas around how people consume content rather than job titles. One I called the **Lifestyle Dev**: scrolling in their free time, not learning, not debugging. They want entertainment, they want to feel part of something, and they reshare relatable content because that's a way of connecting with others.

That's exactly who these shorts are for. Not the person googling an error at 2pm. The person on the sofa at 11pm who sees the force-push video, thinks *oh no, that's me*, and sends it to a colleague.

Here's what I got wrong initially. I assumed the funny stuff was entertainment and community-building happened elsewhere — event recaps, conference footage, come-join-us content. It's the reverse. Community runs on insider understanding: things only people inside the group get. A joke about force-pushing on a Friday afternoon is a membership test. If you wince, you're in.

That's why the meme beats footage of real developers in Berlin. The Berlin clip *documents* a community. The joke lets you *participate* in one, from your sofa, in four seconds, free.

My hesitation: I can't find evidence Google chose this. The channel still describes itself as a place to learn Google technology, which is a different audience entirely. Either a well-aimed strategy, or a team that found something that works and never updated the brief.

## Pillar 3 — The Craft: 3/5

There's a playlist called Dev Quick Tips, and its description states: "built for the developer who wants to work smarter, not harder." Named container, stated audience, stated job. Somebody there knows how to do this.

And then it's one playlist, in a sea of hundreds of unsorted shorts.

![The Dev Quick-Tips playlist on the Google for Developers YouTube channel: twelve curated short videos with a written purpose statement, and 699 views between them.](/g4d-dev-quick-tips-framed.png)

Structure, in [Ann Rockley's](https://xmlpress.net/publications/enterprise-content-strategy/) sense, is a property of the system, not of individual artefacts. One good playlist isn't a system. It's evidence the capability exists and nobody made it the default — a content model defined once and never enforced. That's governance, not design.

What earns real credit is the multichannel handling. Google runs short-form on YouTube, Instagram and TikTok, and the feeds are curated differently rather than cross-posted. That's the expensive, correct approach, and the one most teams skip.

![Google for Developers on YouTube and Instagram side by side, showing differently curated feeds from the same channel.](/g4d-feeds-compared.png)

One correction while I'm here: in my thesis I wrote that Google and AWS were both deliberately avoiding TikTok. No longer true for Google. My own research aged out in twelve months.

## Pillar 4 — The Trust Signal: 3/5

Who's actually talking to you in these videos?

Their name is in the description. Not the title, not on screen. So I don't think it's there to build credibility with the viewer. I think it's for the person in the video: a small piece of recognition that makes it worth showing up again next week.

More interesting is *who* ends up on camera. Most people in these shorts are developers. But the trendiest videos — meme formats, trending audio, the ones that perform — are often fronted by marketing people standing in.

So: technical content gets real practitioners and low views. Funny content gets marketers and high views. Credibility and reach pulling in opposite directions, structurally rather than by accident.

AWS built its short-form around named developer influencers with standing in the community before AWS borrowed it. Whether or not you like that, it's a decision about credibility. Google's looks more like a decision about availability.

To be fair, nobody needs a credential to tell a joke, and trust probably isn't the job here — [the docs](https://developers.google.com/) carry that. But the most-watched face of Google's developer brand is the one with the least developer authority behind it.

## Pillar 5 — The Engine: 4/5

Daily since April 2022, across three platforms with different curation on each, feeding a community hub with a tracked link. That's a real operation, and rarer than it sounds.

This is also where the name in the description makes sense. Three years of daily output needs a constant supply of people willing to be on camera, usually on top of their actual job. A credit costs nothing and gives them a reason to say yes again. Same mechanism as engineer bylines on a tech blog, just weaker, because it isn't visible enough to become a real career asset.

The uncomfortable reading of the same fact: daily posting is exactly what you'd expect from a team optimising for volume because the algorithm rewards volume, with strategy narrated afterwards. Three years in, there isn't a published number in either direction.

## Why it works anyway

**They speak the format.** Trending sounds, correct pacing, jokes with real structure. The force-push video works because someone who understands short-form made it, not because a brand team approved it.

**They don't ask short-form to do long-form's job.** Nobody's teaching Kubernetes in 45 seconds. Stop expecting these to educate and they stop looking like failures.

**They don't ruin it with a pitch.** Technically there's a CTA — a subscribe prompt in the description. That's the whole ask. No trial link, no "try Gemini today," no product wedged into the last three seconds. Most companies can't manage that restraint, and the moment you staple a pitch onto a joke it becomes an ad.

Which brings us back to the parked question.

## The framework that explains it

The [Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/the-955-rule-is-the-new-6040-rule) has a finding known as the 95-5 rule: at any moment, roughly 95% of potential buyers in a category are not in the market. Not researching, not comparing, not buying. About 5% are.

Content aimed at that 95% can't convert them, because there's nothing to convert. Its only job is to build a memory that pays off later — **mental availability**, whether your brand comes to mind when someone finally has the need.

A developer scrolling at 11pm is, by definition, in the 95%. They aren't choosing an AI model tonight. The video isn't failing to sell them anything; selling was never on the table.

That also reframes the missing metrics. If the goal is memory, sign-ups are the wrong instrument — mental availability doesn't surface in conversions for months or years. My thesis reached the same conclusion: when the goal is visibility, you measure views, engagement and reach. Google's silence may be honesty about a hard measurement problem rather than negligence.

**Here's the catch.** Mental availability only pays off if the memory links the brand to the *situation that triggers the need*. "I need an AI coding tool," therefore Google. A joke about force-pushing links Google to developer humour. That's affection, not association, and affection doesn't get recalled at procurement time.

So the videos building the most memory build the least useful kind. And the ones that would build the right association are the ones nobody watches. That's the real critique, and it's more specific than "corporate content is cringe." The strategy isn't wrong. The content that succeeds inside it can't deliver what the strategy needs.

## What I'd steal, and what I'd leave

**Build for identity, not the job queue.** The Lifestyle Dev is a large, under-served audience, and identity content gets reshared in a way tutorials never will. A joke that works as a membership test does more community work than any amount of event footage.

**Curate per platform.** Cross-posting one cut to three feeds is the cheapest shortcut available and the most visible.

**Keep the ask small.** No CTA is defensible if you know why you're making that choice. These stay watchable precisely because they aren't trying to close anything.

**Don't confuse a playlist with a system.** If you can only enforce structure on the content someone happened to care about, you don't have an information architecture.

**Decide who fronts it, deliberately.** If practitioners carry the technical material and marketers carry the reach, your most-seen content is your least authoritative. That might be an acceptable trade. It shouldn't be an accident.

**Pick your measurement before you start.** If the goal is memory, say so and measure reach and recall instead of pretending a funnel exists. Google's problem isn't that it can't prove conversion. It's that from outside, you can't tell whether anyone ever agreed what success looked like.

## The takeaway

Google for Developers serves the Lifestyle Dev: the developer who wants to feel seen for four seconds between other videos. Real audience, legitimate thing to build for.

It just isn't the audience the channel says it's for, and I don't think anyone inside has fully noticed. The description promises community and learning. The feed delivers belonging and entertainment. Close enough to look identical on a dashboard, far enough apart to matter.

The awkward thing about content with no call to action is that you can't tell whether it's working by looking at it. You have to know what it was for. Three years in, the most honest thing you can say is that Google's shorts are very good at being remembered, and nobody has established what they're remembered *for*.
